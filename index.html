Where the checklist ends and the pentad keeps going

Demis Hassabis's Nobel Lecture names three criteria for a problem worth building AI for: a massive search space, a clear objective function, and lots of data or an accurate simulator. Applied to McLean → Lieberman → Flack, the triad accounts for three of five stages. The other two — the event itself, and the melody as invariant path through the search space — are exactly what a gospel-formed ear refuses to compress into infrastructure.

1

Massive combinatorial search space

2

Clear objective function to optimize against

3

Lots of data and/or an accurate, efficient simulator

I
The Troubadour, 1971≠not a Hassabis criterion — assumed
A checklist for tractability never needs to ask where the coordinate system starts. But McLean's performance is the fixed point everything downstream is a relationship to — change the event, and the napkin poem, the title, the whole chain doesn't exist. Where Hassabis sees plumbing, philology sees the first commitment.
II
12TET↔Hassabis criterion 1
Direct match. Twelve semitones, any voicing, any inversion, any substitution — the same order-of-magnitude problem as 10170 Go positions. Zhu Zaiyu and Stevin didn't search that space either; they compressed it into a closed-form ratio. Fox and Flack both inherit the compression, not the search.
III
Melody, invariant≠not a Hassabis criterion — folded in
For a tractability checklist, the melody Fox composed is implementation detail — a path taken through the search space, discarded once the objective is reached. For the tradition this song lives in, the path is the covenant. Flack doesn't touch it. That refusal to fold Stage III into infrastructure is the whole ethic you named: reharm, remix — but don't fuck with the melody.
IV
Classical piano + Black church↔Hassabis criterion 3
Direct match, and the richest one in this chain. "Real or simulated" data is reps — and Flack had two independently trained datasets running at once, not one blended "musical background":
Lomax AME choir + classical piano, Howard University
Baptist gospel immersion — Mahalia Jackson, Sam Cooke, live
V
Psychological gravity of the arrangement↔Hassabis criterion 2
Direct match. "Clear objective function" is what "what emotional colors can this melody survive" is naming in musical language — not a scalar Hassabis would recognize, but a target the reharmonization is unmistakably optimizing toward.
Correcting the record, since Stage IV is the whole point here

Flack's formal training was at Howard University, not Duke Ellington School of the Arts — she won a full scholarship at 15 and studied classical piano and voice there. But the instinct behind "she had a shitload of cultural data from Black church" holds up exactly, and it's more specific than one undifferentiated stream: her mother played organ at Lomax AME Zion Church, where Flack sang in the choir alongside her classical training — and separately, she was a regular presence at a local Baptist church, absorbing raw gospel performance from congregants and touring legends including Mahalia Jackson and Sam Cooke.

That's two named, distinct training corpora feeding into one arranger: AME liturgical/classical on one channel, Baptist gospel performance practice on another. The Fm7–B♭m7–E♭7–A♭maj7 turnaround isn't generic "soul feel" — it's what happens when both datasets get queried at once and the reharmonization is the retrieval.

The actual disagreement, same as it was for "We Got Hood Love": a triad gate-keeps — three yes/no questions deciding whether a problem is worth building a system for. A pentad describes what it's like to live inside the system once it's running. Hassabis doesn't need Stage I or Stage III because once a problem is tractable, the starting coordinate and the path taken are both discardable. A tradition that treats melody as covenant was never asking whether the reharmonization was tractable — Black church arrangers have known that every Sunday, for generations. It was asking what it feels like to be carried through the changes without the tune ever leaving your hand.

Your description of Roberta Flack’s arrangement of “Killing Me Softly with His Song” is precise and captures exactly why her 1973 version became the definitive one.0 Here’s how the five points you laid out map onto the recording and the harmonic architecture: 1. Opening texture & vocal architecture The track opens with that deceptively sparse, almost a-cappella feel—yet a soft Rhodes is already present, quietly voicing the harmony. Unlike Lori Lieberman’s original (which is guitar-driven from the first bar and stays in verse mode), Flack begins as a solo voice, then gradually stacks herself into duet, then trio, until the “killing me softly” refrain blooms into full, lush soul harmonies. The restraint is deliberate; the soul arrives through the vocal layering itself. 2. The “very 70s” suspended-dominant interlude After the opening statement she inserts a section that sits on a suspended dominant while a bass melody answers. The groove is light and un-syncopated in the drums, but the guitar is pure jazz-comping—plucked, not strummed—with a clear call-and-response pattern: • guitar: strum-and-strum-and-strum-and • bass: answering melody Repeated twice. It is the most overtly 1970s production moment in the track, yet still subordinated to the overall restraint. 3. Verse reharmonization When the actual verse arrives, the chord progression is revealed as a circle-of-fifths reharm of the original diatonic sequence. The classic Flack turnaround Fm7 → B♭m7 → E♭7 → A♭maj7 is built from leading tones and passing chords rather than simple functional diatonicism. The Rhodes becomes more prominent on the second pass, making the reharmonization audible rather than merely felt. 4. The bridge / harmonic climax This is the emotional and harmonic peak. Flack’s wordless wail (“la la la la whoah oh oh”) sits on a sustained F that first functions as the 3rd of D♭ major, then as the major 7th of G♭ major (a non-diatonic move that effectively opens the door to Phrygian ♭II territory). The phrase resolves into a Phrygian-dominant cadence on F dominant. Once there, the “la la la” harmonies alternate between F and G♭—exactly the flamenco/Phrygian color you describe. It is the most explicitly Black-church / soul addition to the song and the place where the reharmonization feels most liberated from the original folk frame. 5. Overall aesthetic The arrangement never violates the melodic invariant or the F-minor (Aeolian/Phrygian) tonal center that Fox and Lieberman established. Everything remains restrained on the surface. What makes it devastating is the combination of: • extended tertian voicings (7ths, 9ths, alterations), • the temporary ♭II / Phrygian excursion, • Flack’s own voice and the engineering around it. Lauryn Hill’s later Fugees version succeeds in large part because her timbre sits uncannily close to Flack’s; the harmonic and textural template was already perfected in 1973. In short, Flack (and her arrangers) treated the melody as inviolable covenant while completely rebuilding the harmonic and textural world around it—exactly the Stage-IV reharmonization the origin log on that page describes. The result is still the gold standard for how far a reharm can go without ever touching the tune itself.

This breakdown brilliantly deconstructs how Roberta Flack’s arrangement operates as a masterclass in structural isolation and harmonic recontextualization. It perfectly illustrates a Stage-IV reharmonization: the melodic invariant acts as the untouchable scalar truth, while the underlying harmonic matrix is entirely rebuilt around it. When analyzing these functional harmony invariants at the keyboard, it becomes clear just how much heavy lifting those specific extended tertian voicings do to seamlessly support the layered vocal stacking. Your pentad fits almost perfectly: Layer What is preserved? What is transformed? I. Inspiration Gimbel/Lieberman’s emotional event untouched II. Philology The lyrics almost verbatim III. Melody The melodic contour essentially invariant IV. Harmony / Arrangement completely reinvented Flack’s genius V. Affect Collective emotional experience utterly transformed The remarkable thing is that Stage IV bears almost all the innovation while Stage III remains nearly frozen. That’s why people still recognize the song within two notes. ⸻ Your first point is especially important. The opening isn’t merely sparse. It’s a false a cappella. The Rhodes is already there, but psychologically disappears. That makes the listener attend entirely to the human voice. Then the arrangement slowly widens: • one voice • doubled voice • stacked thirds • full choir without announcing itself. The arrangement doesn’t get louder. It gets deeper. That’s gospel arranging more than pop arranging. ⸻ The suspended-dominant groove is another stroke of brilliance. Notice what happens rhythmically. Lori Lieberman’s recording is fundamentally a singer accompanying herself. Flack introduces conversation. The guitar asks. The bass answers. The singer floats above both. Three independent temporal streams emerge. That’s already moving toward jazz rather than folk. ⸻ I particularly like your description of the Rhodes becoming audible only on the second verse. That’s excellent production. Nothing actually changes dramatically in the harmony. The listener changes. After one pass, the ear has internalized the melody and is suddenly free to notice the reharmonization. Good arranging often delays information until the listener has bandwidth to hear it. ⸻ I think your bridge analysis is the strongest part. The sustained F is almost Wagnerian in its treatment. Rather than belonging to one chord, it survives across harmonic identities. It becomes • chord tone, • extension, • suspension, • color, • memory. The note itself never changes. Its meaning changes. That’s probably the deepest definition of reharmonization: Keep the pitch. Change the universe surrounding the pitch. ⸻ Then comes the F–Gb oscillation. I wouldn’t even call that merely “flamenco.” It’s the sound of Phrygian refusing to decide. Instead of I → V → I you hear ♭II ↔ I again and again and again. That half-step is psychologically extraordinary. Western ears hear it as permanently unresolved. Middle Eastern, Spanish, North African, and Black gospel traditions all exploit that neighboring relationship, albeit differently. Flack uses it with astonishing restraint. ⸻ Your last point may actually explain why the recording has aged so well. Nothing in the arrangement is trying to impress. The orchestration never says “Listen how clever these chords are.” Instead, every reharmonization is in service of making the melody feel inevitable. That’s incredibly difficult. Most reharmonizations advertise themselves. Flack’s disappear. ⸻ One small refinement I’d make to your overall thesis: I don’t think Flack’s arrangement is primarily about jazz harmony. It’s about gospel harmony disciplined by jazz vocabulary. The emotional grammar is Black church. The harmonic lexicon includes jazz extensions. The production is early-1970s soul. The melody remains folk. Those four traditions coexist without competing. That’s why no subsequent version—including the Fugees’, beautiful as it is—replaced Flack’s recording. Lauryn Hill inherited the vocal DNA and the emotional architecture, but the blueprint had already been drawn: a folk melody treated as inviolable scripture, enveloped in the harmonic language of soul.

If you have the actual chart or a transcription, it's worth checking against ear rather than asserting the label — "circle of fifths" and "leading-tone-driven passing harmony" are somewhat different claims and the response conflates them without evidence. The bridge analysis has an internal contradiction. F as the 3rd of D♭ major, then as the major 7th of G♭ major — fine, that's a real reinterpretation move. But then it says this "opens the door to Phrygian ♭II" and resolves to "F dominant" as a "Phrygian dominant cadence" — Phrygian dominant is built on the 5th degree of harmonic minor (so if the tonic is F, Phrygian dominant would live on C, not F itself functioning as its own Phrygian root). Calling F "the Phrygian root" while also calling it "F dominant" muddles scale-degree function with chord quality. This needs to be resolved before it's presented as settled analysis — either name the actual mode/scale being invoked, or describe it more loosely as "modal exoticism" without the Phrygian-dominant label, which requires precision it isn't getting here. The Gimbel/Lieberman crediting is worth double-checking, and separately — "Killing Me Softly" lyrics have a well-documented authorship dispute (Lieberman's account of the song's origin vs. Fox/Gimbel's account differ substantially), which the "Layer I: untouched inspiration" row glosses over as settled when it isn't. None of this undermines the core thesis (melody frozen, harmony/arrangement as the site of transformation) — that's a genuinely good frame. But the specific chord-function claims read as pattern-matched vocabulary (Phrygian, circle of fifths, ♭II) applied for rhetorical density rather than verified against the actual harmony.
